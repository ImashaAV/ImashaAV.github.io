# Gestalt Laws and Preattentive Processing: Analysing World Poster

### Published: 2 August 2026

## Objective
In this exercise I selected a data visualisation published on the web and analysed it 
through visual perception theory. The goal was to identify a feature of this 
visualisation that demonstrates preattentive processing, explain a Gestalt law it shows, 
and evaluate the visual accuracy of one of its quantitative variables.
From this exercise I understood how data visualisations are purposely created in such a 
way that guides human attention and communicates information effectively, and also
where design choices can introduce certain inaccuracies.

## 1.	What aspect of your chosen visualisation first grabbed your attention?
The feature that first grabbed my attention was the large, brightly coloured bubbles in the chart. Particularly the ones like China and India stands immediately because of their size. This relates to preattentive processing where certain visual properties like colour and size are detected by our brain almost instantly. This happens before we deliberately scan the chart. Because bubble size directly means population and colour means world region, my eye was drawn first to the largest, most visually dominant bubbles without having to read any axis or country names. This is how visualisation uses preattentive features to guide the viewer’s attention to the most significant data points before analysis begins. 


## 2.	Explain one Gestalt law that your chosen visualisation demonstrates
The Gapminder World poster I chose demonstrate the Gestalt Law of Similarity. The law states that, visual elements sharing common properties like colour, shape or size are perceived by our brain as belonging in the same group. They don’t have to necessarily be physically located next to each other. Looking at the above visualisation, every country is colour-coded according to its world region. For example, countries in America are in green while countries in Africa are in blue. Even though bubbles representing countries from the same region are scattered across the chart based on their life expectancy and income levels, the shared colour lets the viewer instantly recognize them as countries belonging to a certain region. This shows how similarity in colour can create a sense of grouping when there isn’t spatial 
proximity. 

## 3.	Choose one of the quantitative variables visualised and discuss its visual accuracy
One quantitative variable visualised that I want to discuss is population, which is represented by bubble size or area. As I mentioned above, this encoding is effective for preattentive processing allowing viewers to instantly identify which countries have the largest populations without reading any labels. But it isn’t the most visually accurate method to represent quantitative data. This is because our eyes and brain don’t perceive area the way it’s mathematically calculated. When we look at two circles, we judge their size based on their radius or width, rather than actually comparing the surface area of each. Because area grows with the square of the radius, a circle only needs be about 1.4 times wider to actually double the area. But because our brain isn’t naturally doing this calculation, a bubble that is truly twice the size of another does not look twice as big to us. As a result, when comparing two bubbles, viewers underestimate how much larger one population is compared to another. This means that while bubble size does a good job in drawing attention to population differences at a glance, it could cause viewers to underestimate the true difference between population of countries. A more accurate alternative would be a bar chart using length as population which would allow for more precise comparisons but that would lose the immediate readability that this bubble chart provides.
